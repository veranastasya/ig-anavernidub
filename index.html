What the numbers say

The account went viral and gained two followers. 38,104 people reached — seven times any previous week on this page — 5,355 interactions, 742 shares on one Reel. Net follower change: +2. Instagram attributes 8 follows to these posts, which means roughly six accounts left in the same period. This is not a bad week; it is the clearest possible statement of the problem that has been building here for a month.

The arithmetic, laid out. The Sep 10 Reel reached 26,548 strangers and produced 4 follows — one per 6,600 people. The REVD studio Reel reached 1,110 and produced 1 — one per 1,110. Per person reached, the small fitness post converted six times better than the viral one. Every week this dashboard has run, the same relationship has held: the further a post travels, the less it converts, because what makes it travel says nothing about what this account is for.

The good news is real and worth naming. 112 saves and 742 shares on one Reel is not a fluke, and it is the first hard evidence that you can make something people actually keep. The craft is not the constraint. The subject is: a vacation Reel with a fitness hashtag gets saved because it is enjoyable, not because it is useful, and nobody follows an account for a feeling they already got for free.

The carousels this week were friends-and-family content. 96.9% and 97.8% follower views, 22–27% engagement per reach, zero saves, one and seven profile visits. They performed well at what they are. They are not acquisition and should not be read as a carousel result.

What that means for the next post. You now have 38,000 people who have seen your face this week and 2,113 who follow you, and the gap between those numbers is the entire opportunity. The step-count Reel published minutes ago is the right instinct and the right test — the first useful thing after six days of travel. The thing to fix, which the REVD post also missed, is that not one post this week was saveable for a reason other than entertainment. Every follow this account has ever earned came from something a person wanted to keep.
